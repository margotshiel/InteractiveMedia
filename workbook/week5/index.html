case studies

Vera Molnar

Vera Molnar was an early adopter or computational thinking and generative/computer based art. Up until her death in 2023, she was still producing computer-assisted art works, this time with artificial intelligence. I decided to look at one of her earliest works, Machine Imaginaire (1959) and her final work AD.VM.AV.IA, a series of 16 images co-produced with a customised artificial intelligence. Machine Imaginaire was really exciting to me - she created a series of rules/conceptual framework that mimicked computer thinking/algorithms before she had access to actual computers. The products of this framework are really beautiful line drawings. I particularly love the Letters from my Mother screenprints that turn handwriting into an unreadable block of scribbles. I love the idea that these rules can enrypt personal information.

The final piece that she worked on, in collaboration with an artificial intelligence, used Albrecht Dürer's engraving Melencolia I as a starting point. She used the AI as a way to 'adventure' to the other side of the engraving, and see the reflective three dimensional shape from behind. The outputs come from an AI trained on the style of Dürer's engravings and were selected by the artist based on their aesthetic qualities. I think these are beautiful! And the idea of AI as a tool for adventure is really inspiring to me, and has shifted my perspective.

sticky fingers by Alwayscodingsomething

In my exploration of open processing I also came across this sketch by Alwayscodingsomething called ml5.js sticky fingers grab to stretch. This sketch is so so much fun ahahahah! The canvas is completely taken up by a webcam video and the interaction is that you can use your hand to warp the image by pressing your fingertips together and pulling them around the screen. I had a lot of fun with this and got some very strange looks at uni which is always a big plus.

This sketch's code is written, as the title would suggest, in ml5.js, which I learned is a library to make the integration of machine learning into projects easier and more approachable. I read through the code that Alwayscodingsomething wrote for this sketch and could not really make a heap of sense of it, but looking at the ml5.js website and library, it looks like they're using the hand skeleton finger tracking capabilities that the library has.

Richard Prince

Prince's work is questions the American identity deeply, by using rephotography, photomontage and by placing found works and original photographs side by side, without distinction. He looks at mass media and our perception of imagery in different environments. By placing an appropriated advertising image into the gallery space, how does our perception of the content change? Can we look at images without bringing our pior understanding and preconveienced notions of their meanings into our assessment of them?

While I do find the questions, processes and ideas behind his work really interesting - especially in terms of Walter Benjamin's theory of 'aura', authority and ownership, I do not feel affected by his actual visual output. Aside from some of the cowboy photographs and some of his works that involve text (like his untitled 2009 collage which reads: I just married a two headed lady), I really don't feel that these have much affect on me at all. I think this could be due to the lack of aura!

Dries Depoorter

Dries Depoorter is a Belgium artist that handles themes as privacy, artificial intelligence, surveillance & social media. Depoorter creates interactive installations, apps and games. (via the artist's website).

I really love Depoorter's work, both in terms of his themes and in terms of execution and visual language. When I saw Border Birds in our class, I was immediately in love - the artwork uses AI (Nvidia Jetson Nano) and open surveillance footage to capture images of bird crossing borders. Created in collaboration with Bieke Depoorter (the artist's sister), a portion of the proceeds from this work also go to supporting the European Network of Migrant Women and the Red Cross supporting refugees. The images of the birds that have been captured are gainy and imbuded with movement and freedom. They are really beautiful.

I also love Shirt which is a black tshirt embroidered with the word 'shirt' and a number that corresponds to the price the buyer paid for the item. Starting at 0 euros, with every purchase the shirt's price increases by one euro. I really like this artwork as a response to branding and fashion, and the way that pricing in the fashion industry is almost never reflective of the actual value of an item. Unlike other clothing items, this shirt makes explicit just how arbitrary lots of pricing is.

Aram Bartholl

I started by looking at Aram Bartholl's 2010 performance art piece 15 seconds of fame after we discussed it in class. This piece was made spur of the moment, when the artist, who was sitting in a coffee shop, noticed the google maps camera going past. They ran outside and waved and motioned at the camera. They were captured and immortalised on the google maps streetview, available for anyone to see. I certainly get a kick out of this piece.

The documentation of this piece is really interesting to me as well; the images from the streetview are available as prints, or can be access by any streetview user, or can be watched in a screen recording of a streetview user. I often find the documentation of my work to be the hardest part of an artwork, and though I have come up with strategies (scanning works, taking naturalistic video of the work in situ), I always love seeing how other people attack this challenge.

The next work I looked at was Dead Drops, which was a series of public interventions where Bartholl inserted USBs into public walls around NYC. These could be accessed by anyone of the street, who could upload or download files to the USB. I really love this intervention. It's so cheeky and magical, and really builds this crossing over of physical and digital community. Aesthetically as well, this piece works really well; the little USB heads sticking out of brick walls are so unassuming and hard to spot, while still definitively standing out from the urban fabric.

This work was extending in 2012 with DVD Dead Drop which is described on the artist's website as follows:
For this new commissioned work, artist Aram Bartholl (Berlin, b. 1972) will embed an inconspicuous, slot-loading DVD burner into the side of the Museum, available to the public 24 hours a day. Visitors who find the Dead Drop and insert a blank DVD-R will receive a digital art exhibition, a collection of media, or other featured content curated on a monthly cycle by Bartholl or selected artists. DVD Dead Drop imbues the act of data transfer with a tangibility left behind in a world of cloud computing and appstores, using a medium—the digital versatile disc—that is quickly becoming another artifact of the past.
