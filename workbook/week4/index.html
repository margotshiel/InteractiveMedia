pseudo-code

I misunderstood this task! We were asked to draw an image using simple geometry (squares, circles, lines etc) and then write a set of instructions to explain how to draw this. These were meant to be written in plain English, as they were then passed on to a class mate who attempted to follow them and produce our drawing without having seen the original. I didn't realise that we were writing instructions for another human and so I wrote mine in a kind of pseudocode; a step towards writing the lines of code that is not language specific. This was obviously confusing for my classmate and so her recreation of my original bunny drawing is a little more abstract than I had hoped! But that's okay :/

The purpose of this exercise was to help us understand what it is like for the computer to have to read and interpret our code, and to highlight the importance of specifiy and clarity in our language. Aside from being a really interesting activity in the context of coding, this exercise was really interesting to me just as a demonstration of how communication is so easily muddled and confused. We are not precise creatures, and it was a reminder that unless something is explicitly stated, you cannot expect it to be understood.

case studies

Yoko Ono

We were introduced to Yoko Ono's incructional artworks during class in week 4. We looked at her work Snow Piece which instructs the subject to "Think that snow is falling", and creates an experience rather than an artefact as the artwork. This artwork can be viewed/perceived/experienced by anyone who reads her words and follows the instructions. Simultaneously, the words themselves; their typesetting, and poetic quality, as well as the actual paper that they have been printed on, could also be considered an artefact.

I really love Yoko Ono - my knowledge of her is mostly through The Beatles, and her iconic typographic collaboration with John Lennon, War is Over! If you want it - and discovering more of her algorithmic/instructional work this week has been really exciting. These works do really remind me of poetry, or fiction or music, in that, the art experience can occur anywhere and anytime, without a specific, one of a kind, artefact.

If we take Snow Piece as an example, her instructional artworks contain an idea or concept that can be repeated time and time again, once the reader has imagined it once. This reminds me of having a chorus, or a particularly beautiful line of dialogue stuck in your head. I think this challenging of the line between 'visual' (I'm using the term very loosely here!) art, music, poetry, or even just thought experiments, is really fascinating.

Samuel Monnier

Samuel Monnier's works are algorithnmic art pieces. Fascinated by the complexity and density of patterns, colours and fractals that make up the world around us, Monnier's work uses algorithms (sets of unambiguous rules) to produce more complex patterns and imagery than could have ever been created by the human hand alone.

As he describes in the artist statement on his website, he uses a technique "called pattern piling. Copies of a simple pattern are created at various size scales and are merged together. More recent images are obtained by the iteration of certain conformal maps of the sphere having everywhere dense chaotic orbits." These techniques produce images that are filled to the brim with their own strange system. To me, they look like Persian rugs, or bacteria under a microscope.

To be honest, these works definitely connect with me less than say Yoko Ono's or Sol LeWitt's, in large part because I have to work to get past the initial visual presented. While they are beautiful patterns, I find looking at Monnier's works much less interesting than thinking about his process. For example, his extension of Mondrian's works, his 'fractal' Mondrians are puite powerful to me in theory - take the work of this iconic artist, which is essentially just a set of rules and then push them to their limit through the use of a computer. But the actual result of following this process... I find boring! This is because at such an exaggerated scale, I think the colours and lines lose all of their impact. It is also, probably, effected by the medium of the screen, rather than the canvas, through which I am engaging with these works. I do find them pretty fascinating though, and I have long believed in the importance of process to a work's meaning and value.

Don't touch me by noel

I decided to have a little explore of the artworks on open processing and came across this particle sphere called Don't touch me. by noel. I just picked it at random from their featured sketches but I really love it! It is a collection of animated particles that seem to spin in the shape of a sphere. Their movement is constant, but can be disrupted through the user's mouse interaction. When you come close to, or hover over the sphere, the particles will run away from the cursor, so you cannot touch them.

What I really love about this sketch is that the particles do not just move away from the user at a consistent speed, they react to your speed. If you move slowly, they will creep away from you, but if you move fast they will spring out in all directions in a mini explosion of movement. This is really fun. I had a look at the code itself to see if I could figure out how noel had achieved this effect, and found that it was (in combination with how they set up 'let' and 'const') this piece of code:

let awayFromMouse = p5.Vector.sub(p.pos, mouse);
let distSq = awayFromMouse.magSq();
if (distSq > 0.1 && distSq < repel_radius * repel_radius) {
let distance = sqrt(distSq);
awayFromMouse.normalize();
// natural falloff
let repel = repel_strength * (1 - distance / repel_radius);
awayFromMouse.mult(repel);
p.vel.add(awayFromMouse);
}

I would like to experiment with implementing this in my own sketches, but I am not sure how it would fit into my workbook or final project yet...

Sol LeWitt

Like Yoko Ono, we spoke about Sol LeWitt in week 4's class, and even attempted to draw a Sol LeWitt ourselves, following his written instructions. I found this very difficult! My head was spinning! See my entry in this week's 'activities' section. LeWitt was a radical conceptual artist, whose work had a singular focus on idea and concept, and dicussed the notion that each step of the production of an artwork, could itself be seen as a work of art. "Those that show the thought process of the artist are sometimes more interesting than the final product." (LeWitt, 'Paragraphs on Conceptual Art', 1967)

His works almost entirely consisted of instructions as to how a drawing or sculpture should be produced, rather than the drawing itself. The printed instructions, the fax sent to the gallery, these are what comes to be understood as Sol LeWitts, rather than the images produced by following them. This leads to questions of ownership/authorship. When comparing LeWitt and Ono, LeWitt's works feel more like a puzzle or challenge rather than things of beauty and life. I think this may just be my own subjectivity - I prefer reading poetry to doing maths. But I do see how LeWitt's work and mindset/way of thinking could be informative as I create my own computational/generative/algorithmic works.

Piet Mondrian

I looked into Piet Mondrian more, after looking at Samuel Monnier's 'Truchet' tiled versions of his iconic paintings. This led me down a rabbit hole of researching Neoplasticism and the De Stijl movement. These hard and fast rules about colour, line, balance and shape obviously lend themselves beautifully to the creation of an algorithm, system or code and yet, I find myself so much more affected by the work of Mondrian than I do Samuel Monnier.

My consideration of why is gap in affect exists, points to the concept of 'aura' or authorship that I explore in my next entry on Walter Benjamin. But I think it's more than that too - in particular, I think it's the names of Mondrian's artworks! Broadway Boogie Woogie is maybe the greatest title of all time, and I do really believe that it changes the painting. Where, perhaps, his rules and systems seem, and could be, strict or rigid and fundamentally unsilly, Mondrian's titles are what make them come to life, dance on the canvas and endear them to me so so much! I think this could be a good lesson in how to jazz up something quite dry and insert humanity back into the technological. Copywriting is king!

Walter Benjamin

I reread Walter Benjamin's essay The work of art in the age of mechanical reproduction as I was looking into algorithmic art, and in particular, Samuel Monnier's 'fractal' Mondrian's. The depreciation of 'aura' (authenticity, uniqueness, authority, tradition) that Benjamin describes in his essay has only increased with the advent of digital reproductions and the internet. Now, an artwork is not only filtered through photographs and film, but through screenshots of photographs that have been emailed and downloaded and reproduced a hundred times.

Benjamin argues that this loss of aura leads to the practice of art becoming separate from cult/ritual practices, culminating in the doctine of l'art pour l'art. He warns that to strip an artwork of its cultural meaning, without imbuing it with some kind of political meaning, is dangerous, ultimately leading to the Futurists' (Facists') ideal of war as beautiful. I find this essay compelling and convincing, and I do feel scared that AI has again revolutionised the idea of reproduction. It produces images, words and video, by taking from the work of others and reproducing it as something with its own, new aura. How is this aura distinguishable from the aura of an artefact created by a human?

self portrait

As I was tasked with producing a p5.js self portrait in a WIST submission, I had already given this activity a go, and I must say, I think this second attempt is much much more successful! I started by sketching a version of what I wanted to create, using basic geometric shapes and then produced these in p5.js.

This was cute, but it had room to add movement or animation and so I added an 'eye roll' to the pupils which I think is really fun! I used the library reference and the google AI overview to help my produce this piece of code, which oscillates the circle shape horizontally and vertically at the same time. I would like to try and figure out a way to use the cursor to move the pupils...I think it would be cool to create a kind of Mona Lisaeyes following the cursor kind of effect using a set possible range of movement for the pupils, but still having them follow the cursor. The code is as follows:

function setup() {
createCanvas(windowWidth, windowHeight);
background(0);
angleMode(DEGREES); }

function draw() {
//body + head
fill(255, 255, 0);
strokeWeight(2);
triangle(width/4, height, width/2, height/2, 3*width/4, height);
ellipse(width/2, height/2, 550, 600);

//face fill(249, 250, 207);
ellipse(width/2, height/2, 400, 450);

//ears
fill(255, 255, 0);
ellipse(width/3, 250 , 175, 450);
ellipse(2*width/3, 250, 175, 450);

//whites of eyes

fill(255, 255, 255);
circle(3*width/5, height/2, 100);
circle(2*width/5, height/2, 100);

}

Yayoi Kusama sketch

This sketch I animated and practiced using the keyPressed function with a text variable. I think it's fun and a bit overwhelming maybe, but I think that's in the spirit of Yayoi Kusama's work. Originally the heavy stroke weight on the text was kind of an accident, but I actually really like it! I think the blue is great too, and this piece of text definitely has the sense of urgency that I wanted it to! Haha! The code is as follows:

function setup() {
createCanvas(windowWidth, windowHeight);
angleMode(DEGREES);
frameRate(0.75);
let r = random(255);
let g = random(255);
let b = random(255);
background(r, g, b);
textSize(72);
}

function draw() {
//circle
let randx = random(width);
let randy = random(height);
let r2 = random(255);
let g2 = random(255);
let b2 = random(255);
strokeWeight(20);
stroke(0, 0, 255);
fill(r2, g2, b2);
circle(randx, randy, 50);
text('START TYPING NOW OR ELSE!', 200, height/2);
}
