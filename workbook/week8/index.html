activities

inputs/outputs

For this class activity, we made a grid with a series of possible inputs on one axis, and possible outputs on the other. Then in the cell that corresponded to each, we created an invention that could use these inputs and outputs. After filling in as many as we could, we traded with a classmate and then discussed and filled out each other's. I traded with Lauren and we came up with some great ideas for encouraging sleep or relaxation. For both of us, a lot of our pieces were textiles based, or wearable in some way, and like Lauren McCarthy's work, used this proximity to the body to encourage a change in behaviour. I did find myself drawn to the idea of encouraging relaxation, or taking breaks from studying, a lot more strongly than I had anticipate which I think could be a really interesting path to go down...

making an instrument

The final thing that we did using the Arduinos as a group, was to produce a kind of instrument! We used conductive materials including plant matter, potatoes and aluminium foil to produce these funny little buzzers with different pitches, that you could then use to play a tune almost. This was really fun, and again I found the actual setting up of the Arduino pretty straight forward. However, we did have a lot of trouble with just making all of the pathways work consistently. As soon as we figured out one, the rest of the cables would shift slightly in how they were attatched to the bread board and would stop working. This makes me a bit nervous about the prospect of using physical computing in my final work as I can see myself getting very confused and frustrated with things not working.

case studies

Diego Zaks

Karen ann mention Diego Zaks' Murder Machine in the lecture for this week and so I decided to look more into it. Described by the artist as Part infographic, part installation the machine is very basic printer that records the each murder that occurs on the streets of Venezuela. There is one every 19 minutes. The records are printed on a long scroll of paper that accumulates on the floor below the machine in a kind of nest. The typesetting of the information is bare and utilitarian, using a basic sans serif typeface and a simple, left-aligned layout. This stripped back, unembellished declaration of violent death highlights the ways in which we separate these numbers from the actual humans that they represent. When you look at this Murder Machine, you get the distinct feeling that it has gone too far. However, all Zaks is doing here, is reflecting back at us the dehumanisation that we take part in constantly. Furthermore, the machine also acts as a witness for these deaths. It makes note and remembers, when many others do not. I think it is a pretty powerful piece.

Simon Giertz

I love Simone Giertz's Shitty Robots and had a really great time sitting back and watching her youtube videos. Her breakfast robot, which attempts to pour cereal and milk in a bowl before feeding them to Giertz, is a particular favourite. The beer robot and soup robots 'perform' similar functions with similar levels of success (none at all). As someone who really struggles with perfectionism, and finds it hard to embrace the possibilities that are created when things go wrong, I find Giertz's mission to produce robots that are fun, quick and beautifully imperfect, rather than neccessarily functional, makes my heart leap up. This feels like a kind of anticapitalist statement as well; in a world where everything must be seen to be running smoothly, and efficiency and efficacy are prized above all else, to produce something that doesn't need to work or perform, feels revolutionary.

I also like the format of Giertz's documentation as a series of videos on her youtube channel. She presents her works in a combination of vlogs that detail the construction of the robots, and 30ish second videos that just show the final product and how badly it works. I do think that simple videos in natural light, can be the best way to communicate the feeling of an installation or sculptural work that doesn't neccessarily communicate very much through photos alone.

Cloth!! by cloud.cb

This was another little sketch that I found in my exploration of the open processing website. This is super beautiful. Basically a grid is renders with fine white lines on black, which can then be moved by clicking and dragging and the grid will sort of turn into a piece of flowing cloth that slowly returns to its original place after it has been released. By pressing the 'd' key, a user can also delete sections of the cloth, causing it to fall/flutter away. This is deliciously simple and, as a lover of fabric and draping and flow, this really scratches an itch for me. Even the way that you can destroy this piece if you want, and the delicate fibre that the fine lines evoke, all make this sketch feel so light and gentle and fragile. I think it's very beautiful. The code sets variables and uses classes to make achieve this movement and reaction to the mouse and keys pressed.

play

thoughts and pitch for final project

I have been doing a series brainstorming and sketches as I continue to ideate for my final project and I have come to the conclusion that I would like to try and capture a feeling of magic in my final work. Technology is magical. It's crazy that I can speak with someone on the other side of the world in real time, or that I can have a million photos and endless music with me at all times. But as we age, and understand how so of this stuff works, it becomes less magical. In general, most things become less magical...

Until we are about 6 or 7, we don't fully understand the difference between imagination and reality. Paul Harris is a child psychologist and academic. His research reports that when children of this age were shown an empty box and then asked to imagine that there was a puppy in the closed box, they could recognise that there wasn't really one in there when asked. However, when left alone in the room with the box, they would almost always open the box and double check it was empty, just to make sure they hadn't wished a puppy into existence. Similarly, children who were told to imagine a monster in the box would back away from it when left alone. So kids know that, logically, they can't wish things into existence, but they don't neccessarily fully believe this fact. I think this is so so delightful! They really believe in the power of wishing for things! Maybe with technology, I could bring this imagination to life somehow?

I have been considering different formats through which to present this feeling of magic, and I like the idea of making a small, warm, quiet space where we can go back and become childlike and enchanted again. So I have been playing with the idea of a tent/pillow fort-esque play area that could be inviting and mysterious. I'm currently working on my pitch for week 9's class and I am framing the work around this kind of enclosure. I might need to pivot at some point though, and consider something on a smaller scale (maybe using the 'box' from the Paul Harris experiment?) to focus my work and bring it back to something that I can actually produce in the alloted time frame. We shall see!
