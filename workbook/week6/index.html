activities

going through the Arduino box

This activity had us go through the Arduino kit and guess what each item is/does. Becky and I did our best, but were a little off with most of our guess (eg. calling LEDs lights). Then Andy and Karen Ann explained the actual names and uses for each part, and we corrected our original guesses with green pen. This demystified the parts a bit, but I still will definitely need to go back through and re-examine/research each item in order to fully understand the potential uses that they could have for my own independent work. I did enjoy working with Becky a lot as well! This was a good reminder that the final project can also be completed in pairs, which would allow for a larger scope.

turning on LEDs

The second activity that we completed with the Arduino kits, was using the LEDS and buttons to produce a circuit. We began with a diagram to show up how to set up the circuit with a single LED, then attempted to add a second ourselves. The learning curve with these little guys is definitely going to be steep, but it was also so satisfying to get the LED to finally turn on. It took a while for us to get everything working; we needed to change out the level of resistor and then make sure that the button was clicking in properly before we could get everything to work. I still don't think I have quite grasped resistors yet, but I honestly think the only way forward is going to be through with this new skill so I will keep pushing on.

case studies

Bhumi

I have been following this brand that uses PLA, a biodegradable material made from renewable resources like cornstarch and sugarcane, to 3D print bags, clothing and most recently shoes, called Bhumi. Created by Emma Van Engelen, these designs have goals of inclusivity and sustainability and above all they look really crazy and exciting! To me, these organic, 3D printed sculptures look like coral, or plants growing up off the body and transforming the body into a purposefully distorted, sculptural form. They make products to order, to reduce over production and then excess stock can be recycled into filament, which can then be used again. Further, as each design is completely replicable with just a digital file, the goal for the brand is to have a future where pieces can be 3D printed in the same geographical location as where they will be worn, thus reducing transport costs and environmental impacts.

I have only used 3D modelling software quite sporadically, and am definitely not a master at this kind of production, but I have got a lot of experience producing clothing in more traditional ways, and so I feel like this could be a really cool way to stretch my brain and try to visualise designing for the body in a totally different way. I am very impressed by Bhumi's production methods in reference to sustainability, and I think that embracing technology is really the only way that independent labels will be able to continue to operate with cultural spotlights on sustainability, size inclusivity and cost. It does seem like, somehow, at Bhumi, they are doing it all.

Cyberdecks

Cyberdecks have been all over my social media feed and were actually introduced to me by my manager at work. Like me, my manager does lots of fibre arts like knitting, crochet, sewing and embroidery. We always talk about our artworks and she was discussing a new form of creation that she is interested in exploring.

Cyberdecks are tiny, simple computers, built using repurposed electrical parts and often housed in tiny purses, tins, old game consoles etc. What I love about them is that they come from a distinctly feminine view point - the majority of these creators/engineers are women, and there is a real focus on aesthetic and the physical design of the object. They are decorated and ornamental, in clear opposition with Apple's sleek masculine machines.

Generally these decks are capable of a single task. They store text files, record audio or act as tiny kindles. There is a really active community around them which aims to make them super accessible to everyone.

Lauren McCarthy

Lauren McCarthy's work examines how we relate to each other in an increasingly automated world. She is focussed on the relationships between people. As the creator of p5.js, she has also demonstrated her desire to bring code and technology to demographics that have previously be disincentivised to interact with these forms of creation. I love the simplicity and accessibility of the p5.js library so much, and when I saw her wearable/textiles works in our week 6 class, I just knew that I had to look into her further.

Her series of wearable works Tools for Improved Social Interacting is described in an artist statement as follows:
Series of wearable devices that use sensors to condition the behaviour of the wearer to better adapt to expected social behaviours. Exploring the potential for technology to shape how we think, feel, and act, and questioning our social expectations, attempting to better understand the function and worth of them. Are there alternative ways of interacting that leave more space for individual expression, thought, and connection?

These three works uses basic sensors and motors to train the user;
The Happiness Hat trains the wearer to smile more. An enclosed bend sensor attaches to the cheek and measures smile size, a servo motor moves a metal spike into the head inversely proportional to the degree of smile. The smile size data is logged on a microSD memory card for download at the end of each use period.
The Anti-Daydreaming Scarf contains a heat radiation sensor that detects if the wearer is engaged in conversation with another person. While he is, the scarf vibrates periodically to remind the wearer to pay attention and stop daydreaming.
The Body Contact Training Suit requires the wearer to maintain frequent body contact with another person in order to hear normally. If the wearer stops touching someone for too long, static noise begins to play through headphones sewn into hood. A capacitance sensing circuit measures skin to skin body contact via a metal bracelet sewn into the sleeve.

Aside from the technological and social aspects of these projects, I also find the physical, soft garment form in which they come to be really charming and emotionally connecting. We have so many apps and digital tools that supposedly make us into better communicators, but there is something so beautiful about the idea of a garment training your body. The projects have been knitted, which I also find fascinating, as it is such a personal, hand touched craft, where the wearer can really feel and see the number of hours that have been put into something. I find knitting interesting from a mathematical/algorithmic standpoint as well, as lots of colour-work and shaping of garments is communicated through charts and diagrams that basically fill in a series of cells with ones and zeros.

Science Gallery

Visited the Science Gallery with two boys that I babysit, during their school holidays. They really loved the cute, simple video games section and the little room with rgb switches and a screen. I think they liked the tactile nature of these ones - and the older boy (7 years) especially loved the hot dog game, which uses a camera to detect a player's face.

The younger child (4 years) was drawn to the same game as me! This was the Catacombs of Solaris game by Ian MacLarty. "Wander the labyrinth in a colourful perspective maze that plays with your sense of perception. Find your favourite room and take a picture to remember." I was so taken with this weird, seemingly never ending labyrinth of coloured rooms and could honestly have spent all day just 'walking' through this space. There's something about optical illusions that will just never get old for me.

I also, of course, really loved the crochet projects - Minute Lines by Jess Blaustein - and the soft sculptures that we were invited to touch - Touching Wires (Oh...What a joy it is to be held) by Sandy Ma. I added a little line of crochet myself, with the kids' help, and then hugged the soft sculptures for ages! This was so much fun, above all else, and a very easy way to spend a morning with two very energetic children.

initial thoughts on final project

My inital thoughts about my final project, possibly using physical computig and the Telstra Creator Space are still pretty loose at this point. I think it would make sense for me to look at using textiles in some way, perhaps making a wearable like what Lauren McCarthy has done with her Tools for Improved Social Interacting, but I don't yet have a clear picture of what that would look like.

Looking at Bhumi, I am also considering trying to 3D print something (maybe a pair of shoes or a head piece?) but this would require me to go back into 3D modelling software and learn that whole new set of skills on my own... which I could absolutely do, I just need to weigh my options a bit. I like the idea of encouraging dancing, singing or some kind of movement, if I did end up going for a wearable. Lots and lots to consider and lots of different places I could go with this...
